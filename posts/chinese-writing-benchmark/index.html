Every morning, Nina opened the curtains before she put on her leg.

The windows faced the courtyard, where Mrs. Varga beat her rugs on Tuesdays and Fridays regardless of weather. In the opposite building, a boy practiced the trumpet. He knew eleven bars of something cheerful. Nina had counted them in November; by March he had acquired a twelfth.

She sat on the bed and inspected the leather straps. One had begun to split beside the buckle. She would need to take it to the repair shop, where the cobbler always called her madam and spoke to her knee.

For now, she threaded the strap through, pulled it tight, and stood.

The kettle was on the highest shelf. Her sister had put it there during her last visit, along with the good cups and everything else she thought Nina should not be troubled with. Nina brought it down with the handle of a wooden spoon.

Outside, men were replacing the street signs. For three years the corner had been known as the place where the pharmacy burned. Now it was to be Linden Street again, although there were no lindens, and the pharmacist sold buttons from a table beneath his old window.

Nina dressed to go out. Her left shoe needed polish; the right still looked new. She rubbed both.

At the bakery, the girl behind the counter had saved her a heel of yesterday’s loaf.

“For the birds,” she said.

“Yes,” Nina said.

They had maintained this arrangement since January.

She carried the bread beneath her arm and walked toward the theater. The pavement on this route was mostly level. She knew the loose stones, the places where rain collected, the doorway with a brass rail she could hold while pretending to look for something in her bag.

The theater had new glass in its doors. Nina paused to examine it. For months, plywood had covered the entrance, and before that she had avoided this street.

Inside, someone was playing scales.

She had come for her wages. Twice a week she mended costumes in a room beneath the stage, sewing hooks onto bodices whose waists she remembered without measuring. Last Thursday she had found her own name inside a blue tulle skirt. She had unpicked the label and put it in the wastebasket with the broken hooks.

The porter looked up.

“They’re upstairs today. Your room’s flooded.”

“Again?”

“Pipe this time.”

He handed her an envelope, then a small bundle wrapped in cloth.

“Madame asked if you could do these at home.”

Through a gap in the wrapping, Nina saw pink satin.

She tucked the bundle into her bag. At the foot of the stairs, a young dancer was bending over her slipper, trying to knot a ribbon with trembling fingers.

“You’ve twisted it,” Nina said.

The girl looked up. Nina set down her bread.

“Here. Let me.”

Above them, the pianist stopped and began again. Nina folded the ribbon flat against the girl’s ankle. Her fingers made the knot quickly, leaving no ridge to press against the skin.

The Fitting

The prosthetist’s office smelled of resin and coffee, and Marguerite had learned to arrive early so she could watch the others come in. A boy from the mining accident, two men from the front, a woman whose sleeve had been pinned and repinned by someone who did not know how to sew. They looked at her the way people always had, a lingering second longer than politeness allowed, and she let them. There was nothing left in her to arrange against it.

“Weight forward,” Monsieur Delacroix said. “Onto the heel of the good leg first. You are leaning back again.”

She was. She was always leaning back. The new leg was willow and leather and a steel joint that clicked when it locked, and the sound had begun to enter her dreams. In the dreams it was the click of the rehearsal pianist’s metronome, and she was late for her cue.

“Better. Now the other.”

She stepped. The floor of the office had been laid with a strip of parquet, sanded and polished, because Delacroix believed his patients should learn on the surface they would live on. Marguerite had noticed the first day that the boards ran in the same direction as the boards at the Opéra, and had said nothing, and had felt the noticing settle into her like a swallowed stone.

At home her mother had rearranged the flat. The bed was against the wall now, and the chair sat where the barre had been, and the long mirror had gone to the neighbors in exchange for a sack of potatoes. Her mother spoke of the potatoes often and of the mirror never. In the evenings they listened to the wireless, and when the orchestra from Lyon played the second act of Giselle her mother rose and turned the dial, and Marguerite let her, and the two of them sat in the new quiet and shelled peas.

The letters had stopped. For a while there had been a great many, from the company and from the balletomanes who had followed her from the corps to her first Odette, and she had answered each one in a firm, upright hand. Then the company had reopened with Sylvie in the roles that had been hers, and the letters had thinned to the occasional card at Christmas. Sylvie’s card had come in a pale blue envelope with a pressed flower inside. Marguerite had kept the flower and thrown away the card.

“Once more across the room,” Delacroix said. “Then we will stop for today.”

She crossed the room. Fourteen steps. On the twelfth the joint caught and she went down on one hand, and the boy from the mine half rose from his chair, and she waved him back without looking. She got up in the way she had been taught, the good knee first, both hands flat, the head last. It was not so different from the way one rose from a fall in class, except that in class someone always laughed, kindly, and here no one laughed at all.

Outside, it had begun to snow. She stood a while beneath the awning, watching a girl of perhaps nine run the length of the pavement and stop, arms wide, exactly on the curb.

The Weight of Water

The kettle takes four minutes to boil, and Ilse has learned to spend them sitting down.

She spends them at the kitchen table by the window, where the morning comes in grey off the canal and the neighbor’s wash hangs slack in air too still to dry anything. On the table: a cup, a saucer she does not need, and the crutch, laid across the wood like something asleep. She has stopped putting it out of sight when visitors come. That took two years.

The kettle whistles. She rises the way she has taught herself to rise — hands first, the push coming from the arms and the good hip, the whole business conducted without drama, the way one might close a door in a house where someone is ill. There is a rhythm to it. She has never said so aloud, but she has counted it: three beats to stand, four to cross the floor, two to take the handle. Twelve-eight time. In another life she would have found this funny.

The tea steeps. Outside, a boy is teaching a smaller boy to ride a bicycle, running alongside with one hand on the saddle, and the smaller boy is shrieking with the particular joy of a body doing something it did not know it could do. Ilse watches until they turn the corner. Then she watches the corner.

At half past nine she goes down to the shop for bread. Fourteen steps, and the landlady’s cat on the eighth, which she has come to think of as a courtesy, a marker, something to expect. Mrs. Beck at the counter always has the loaf wrapped before Ilse reaches her, always says there you are, dear, always finds some reason not to watch her go back out the door. Kindness in this city has gotten very good at looking elsewhere. Ilse takes the bread and says thank you and does not mind. Or minds in a way she has filed somewhere, along with a great many things, in a drawer she opens rarely and never all the way.

The long way home passes the theatre. She takes it perhaps twice a month, no more, and never admits to herself beforehand that she has chosen it. The building is still shut, the west wall still gone, and through the gap you can see straight into the rehearsal hall — the mirrors mostly shattered, the barre still bolted to the wall at the height it was always bolted, forty inches, a height her hand knows in the dark. Rain gets in. There is grass coming up through the floor near the piano, which someone stripped for firewood in the second winter. It is a strange thing to feel grateful about, that they took the piano and left the barre.

She stands there the length of a cigarette she does not smoke.

Then home, and the tea gone cold, and the afternoon, and the bread. In the evening the woman upstairs plays records, and some nights it is Chopin, and on those nights Ilse sits very still in her chair with her one foot flat on the floor, and her hands on the arms of the chair, and she does not move at all. She is not, she would tell you, remembering anything.

She is listening. There is a difference. She has had a long time to work out what it is.

The Company of the Mariinsky sent flowers every year on the anniversary, and every year Vera left them in the stairwell for Mrs. Denisova downstairs, who liked lilies and did not ask questions.

She had trained her hands to do what her legs no longer volunteered for. This was the first useful thing she learned after the hospital in Kharkiv, in the long grey month when the doctors kept calling the stump “the site,” as though it were a piece of real estate and not the place where, at nineteen, she had ended. Her hands, meanwhile, had never forgotten fifth position. She caught herself doing it at the sink sometimes, wrists curved over a colander of potatoes, elbows lifted just so, and she would stop and feel foolish and go on peeling.

She taught now, three afternoons a week, in a studio above a shoe repair shop where the floor sloped half an inch toward the street and every girl in the beginner class blamed her own clumsiness on it. Vera let them. She sat on a tall stool with her cane hooked over one knee and called out corrections in the flat, carrying voice she had once used to be heard over an orchestra, and she did not tell them that she could see, with more precision than any of them could yet manage, exactly where their weight was wrong, because she had spent two years relearning weight from the ground up, this time with only one leg to argue with gravity.

Local people admired her for it, in that regretful, particular way people admire the survivors of newsworthy disasters — the shelling of the Semyonov Theatre had made the papers as far as Moscow, four dancers dead, the roof come down mid-performance during, of all things, the mad scene from Giselle, which she had always privately thought absurd and now found unbearable to hear mentioned. Neighbors nodded to her in the stairwell with a special gentleness, and shopkeepers rounded down her change, and none of it was what she wanted, though she had stopped, mostly, minding.

What she minded was smaller and stupider than that. She minded that she could no longer reach the top shelf without the step-stool, because balance on one leg and one crutch did not extend upward the way it used to. She minded the particular sound the prosthetic made against the tram’s metal floor, a hollow knock that strangers pretended not to hear. She minded, most of all, a recurring and entirely private grievance: that after the explosion, after the hospital, after the letters of sympathy and the small government pension and the newspaper photograph she refused to look at, the thing she missed with the sharpest, stupidest ache was not the applause, not the roles, not even the leg itself, but a single unremarkable Tuesday in October of the year before the war reached the city, when she had run down four flights of stairs two at a time because she was late for company class, and had not known, could not have known, that her body would ever be any other way.

She still ran the stairs in her mind sometimes. In the dream she was always almost late.

The wood was warm from the hallway radiator. She’d chosen the studio because the large mirror had been removed long ago, taken down by the landlord who said nobody needed to see themselves these days. She’d nodded and paid extra for the privacy.

The barre was positioned now at the right height for her left hand. She stood, weight distributed between her left foot and the cane she’d propped against the piano bench—purchased from a medical supply shop in a neighborhood where no one knew her before the war, and not after. The cane had a rubber tip, nearly silent on the floor. She’d sanded it smooth when the seams began to catch on her stockings.

Her right leg was a story she didn’t tell.

The music began. She moved her right arm to fifth position, a motion that no longer signified anything, and began the plié she could do. The barre held her steady. Her working leg bent and straightened, bent and straightened—not the grand movements of her life before, not the soaring leaps that had earned her roles she would never dance again, but something. A reminder, perhaps, or merely a habit her body refused to abandon.

In the street below, a child called out to his mother. Margot didn’t look at the window. She’d learned not to watch other people move freely; it had a way of sharpening old disappointments into fresh ones.

She shifted her weight to her left side, released the barre, and stood alone for five seconds. Two legs, the world had said, as though she’d simply misplaced one. They’d been kind about it in the hospital—the nurses, the doctors, even the other patients who still had their pairs intact. One had told her, with what she recognized as genuine optimism, that she could still have a normal life. The girl meant well. She’d meant choreography, perhaps, or teaching. The girl had not meant this: a studio rented under a false name, a teacher from the academy who came on Thursdays to work with her behind a locked door, movements that existed only in the space between what she’d been and whatever it was she was becoming.

The record continued. She moved through the positions she could approximate, building small architectures of balance and control in this room where no one watched, where failure was private and silence was hers alone.

Outside, the city had moved on. She remained, suspended in the music, making something from what remained.
