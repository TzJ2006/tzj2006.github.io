Sixteen models, for the same Chinese writing task

August 26, 2026 · 35 min

The same prompt, 4 local Ollama models + 12 cloud models from three Agent CLI, written again each time.
Below is the entire original output, along with speed, VRAM, latency, and token data recorded during each run.

Task#

请用中文写一个约500字的小说开头，以一位在战争爆炸中失去一条腿的芭蕾舞演员为主角，通过描写她在战后生活来侧面体现出她的挣扎。

Running Conditions#

Sixteen models receive exactly the same prompt, without any prefix or system prompt.

Local (Ollama) — RTX 5090 / 32 GB. POST 127.0.0.1:11434/api/chat, stream: false.
No sampling parameters are specified; temperature, num_predict, and context all use the default values from the model Modelfile and ollama.
(ollama ps shows that the context for all four models is 65536, and the KV cache in VRAM is allocated according to this size).
Each model first ollama stop clears the VRAM before running, so the loading time is cold start.
The peak VRAM access(nvidia-smi) minus the desktop baseline (2.5–3.3 GB).
Speed is directly read from the response body(eval_count / eval_duration), without HTTP and Python overhead.

Cloud (Agent CLI) — codex exec, claude -p, cursor-agent --print,
all run serially, --output-format json; the working directory is an empty temporary folder.
Total time is measured by(perf_counter) over the entire process; the number of tokens and API time are read from the usage field in each JSON.

Each model runs once. Sampling is random, and the results and speeds vary. The table below is given as magnitudes.

Two facts affecting the metrics#

  1. The two speed tables cannot be compared. The input for local models is just this prompt itself (50–59 tokens);
    cloud models use a coding agent CLI, which includes their respective system prompts and tool definitions,
    so the input tokens in Table 2 range from 15,000 to 20,000.

  2. The JSON of Claude Code in(input_tokens) shows a single digit, not the actual length,
    so that column is empty for it. Codex and Cursor report the full input.

  3. The running conditions for Fable 5.1 are different from those of the other Claude Code models. It was added later:
    --effort high is explicitly specified, while the other three Claude Code models use the default effort of the CLI.

After the rain stopped, Lin Wan pushed the window open a little. The cart selling soy milk downstairs came out again, the sound of the iron bucket hitting the ground similar to the sound of the heating pipes behind the theater three years ago.

She sat on the edge of the bed, smoothing the silicone cover on her prosthetic. The motion was slow, like tying shoelaces before rehearsal—only now, with nothing under her knee, the shoelace was empty.

The kettle made a sound from the kitchen. She stood up against the wall, her left leg landing first, then her right leg. Her right leg remembered every curve: toes spread out, heel in. But the prosthetic had no curve, only a slight “click” from the metal joint, creating an unfamiliar rhythm on the wooden floor.

She made a bowl of noodles without onions. Before, she hated the smell of onions, saying it would wash away the stage makeup. Now, with only her own in the kitchen, onions or not, it was the same.

The afternoon sun slanted into the living room. In the corner stood a dusty dance shoe, the pink fabric faded to gray, the tip still in its shape from the last time she stood on tiptoe. She didn’t look at it, but knew it was there, as if knowing the calendar on the wall had turned to September—the performance season should have started.

The phone on the coffee table vibrated once. It was a reminder from the rehabilitation center: physical therapy at three in the afternoon. She typed “okay” and put the phone away.

Three o’clock came, and she changed into clothes for going out. In the mirror, she wore loose knitted pants, unable to see the outline of her left leg. She raised her chin against the mirror—a habit before going on stage. The person in the mirror looked back at her, eyes calm, but the corners of her mouth slightly tightened.

She picked up her bag and stopped at the door for a second.

The children’s laughter came from downstairs, running across the stairs, then farther away. She took a deep breath and pushed the door open. The air after rain had the smell of soil and osmanthus. She walked down the steps, one step, then another. The rhythm was unstable, but she didn’t stop.

The stone steps were still wet after the rain. Anna supported herself on the rail, probing the prosthetic first, the rubber bottom slipping slightly on the moss. She stopped for a moment, waiting until the empty sound rising from her knee disappeared, then put the other foot on top.

The bread shop lights were already on. She passed by this window every day. The glass reflected her: short hair, old coat, the unyielding outline beneath the skirt. The window displayed protein cake, white like the walls of the rehearsal hall before. She didn’t enter. The coins in her pocket only lasted half a loaf of rye.

The studio was on the third floor. The elevator was broken for three months, no one repaired it. She counted the steps: thirty-seven, thirty-eight. At the thirty-ninth level, the prosthetic made a soft “click,” as if there was sand in her bone. The door was open. The mirror was still there. The mirror never left the dance classroom, even though it had been converted into a mending shop. In the corner were military coats given by others, sleeves worn, collar blackened. Anna sat down, placing the needle and thread box on her leg—on that still-leg.

She sewed buttons for the children. The children didn’t dance. They came to pick up the repaired pants, looked at her skirt, and sometimes asked: “Auntie, why don’t you show the other one?” She smiled and cut the thread. The thread was sticky, like the smell of cutting the ribbon before.

At noon she opened the window. Someone on the opposite roof was drying bedsheets, the wind blowing the white cloth up, slightly resembling a tutu. Anna’s hand stopped in mid-air. She didn’t grab it. She just watched the cloth fall, wrinkled into a ball, and another hand smoothed it back.

There was also a pair of old toe shoes in the drawer. The tips were soft, and there was a thin layer of gray in the shoe box. She opened them occasionally, not to wear them, but to confirm they were still there. After confirming, she closed them. Like swallowing a sentence back.

In the evening, someone downstairs played old records. It was “Swan Lake.” She didn’t close the window. The prosthetic leaned against the chair leg. She tapped the table edge with her finger, once, twice. To the part that should have been spinning, the finger stopped. Someone passed by outside the window, their laughter soft. She finished the record, turned off the light, folded the empty trouser leg, and put it in the drawer—not to hide it, just to let it sleep.

She still had to go to the third floor tomorrow. Still count the steps. Still thread through the button holes. Life didn’t explode, just continued. Continuing itself was already heavy.

Lesson 1#

Before dawn, Lin Wan woke up. Her right leg hurt under the blanket, below her knee—the doctor called it phantom limb pain. She didn’t touch it; she knew there was only empty sheets there.

The bakery downstairs began to smell. The building was rebuilt after the war, with thin walls, and she could hear the owner opening the rolling curtain. Three years ago, this street was a wasteland. Three years ago, behind the theater at the end of the street, she stuffed soft padding into her toe shoes.

She sat up, first putting on the prosthetic, then the pants. It took her three months to get this order right. The prosthetic was provided by the government—hard plastic, with skin reddening at the joints. She padded it with an old silk cloth from a costume.

Her breakfast was a boiled egg and half a cup of milk. She ate slowly. She used to eat slowly too, to control weight; now it was because there was nothing to rush.

At eight o’clock, she went to the community cultural station to teach. There were over a dozen children, the oldest ten years old. The parents didn’t care if she taught well, just needed a place to leave their kids for two hours. She didn’t care either—she needed the pay.

The classroom floor was concrete, covered with a thin plastic mat. She had the kids stand in a line, holding onto the wall.

“Tuck your heels together, spread your toes.”

The children did it awkwardly. A little girl stood on tiptoe, wobbled slightly, and laughed out loud. Lin Wan watched her wiggling ankle for a long time.

“Teacher, why don’t you demonstrate?”

“My leg is inconvenient,” she said without pausing.

“Did you do it before?”

She didn’t answer, turned to play music. It was Act II of Swan Lake, the tape a bit old and the sound muffled. When the intro started, her right hand lifted involuntarily, forming a half arc, then stopping in mid-air. She lowered it, pressed against her pants pocket—inside was an old program sheet folded four times, the edges softened. She didn’t take it out.

“Do it again,” she said, “tuck your heels together, spread your toes.”

Outside the window, on the rebuilt street, someone was flying a kite.
